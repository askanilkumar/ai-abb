1 · Prompt vs Spec Layer 2

Vibe coding means asking AI to build something with a short, loose request. It is fast, and often good enough for a prototype. But AI fills every gap in your request with a sensible guess — and your business rules are exactly the things it cannot guess.

Spec-driven development means writing a short specification first and giving it to the AI. A good spec has six parts:

PartQuestion it answers
PurposeWhat decision or job does this support?
UsersWho uses it, and what do they already know?
Business rulesThe numbers and conditions only your team knows
Acceptance testsExamples with the correct answer — how we will check the AI's work
Out of scopeWhat it must not do
Output formatHow the result must look

What happened in the background — six steps, two of them AI

1 Read the PDF → 2 Chunk it into pieces → 3 Embed each piece into numbers that capture meaning (AI) → 4 Search for the 3 pieces closest to the question → 5 Prompt = pieces + question + rules → 6 Answer (AI).

In plain words: the model never reads your PDF. It only sees the three pieces the search found. If the search picks the wrong pieces, even the best model gives a wrong answer. That is why question 3 often fails.

Embeddings, vector store and semantic search — try it yourself

Three words you will hear in every RAG discussion. Here they are with one small, drawable example from the Axion HR policy.

1 · Embedding = turning text into a list of numbers that captures its meaning — like GPS coordinates, but for meaning. Texts that mean similar things get similar numbers, even if they use different words ("vacation" and "earned leave").
2 · Vector store = a table that keeps every chunk of text next to its numbers. In your Lab 2 app it is a simple list in memory; in production it is a database such as Azure AI Search or PostgreSQL with pgvector.
3 · Semantic search = turn the question into numbers too, then find the chunks whose numbers are closest. It searches by meaning, not by matching words.
4 · Cosine similarity = how "close" two meanings are, measured by the angle between their arrows. Same direction = 1.00 (same meaning). At right angles = 0.00 (unrelated).

How is cosine similarity calculated? (optional)

For two arrows a and b: cosine = (a₁×b₁ + a₂×b₂) ÷ (length of a × length of b). It only cares about the direction of the arrows, not their length — so a long paragraph and a short question about the same topic still match. With 1,536 numbers the formula just has 1,536 terms instead of 2.

In plain words: the AI does not "search for words". It turns every chunk and your question into points on a meaning map and picks the nearest ones. That is why "vacation" finds "earned leave" — and also why a question that needs two chunks that are far apart on the map can miss one of them. The next section shows exactly that.

3 · Agents + Guardrails Layers 4 and 5

An agent is a model with a job description, tools it can ask to use, and memory of the conversation. A multi-agent system splits a job between several agents, each with its own instructions and tools — like a team. The model never touches data directly: it asks for a tool, and our code runs it.

Guardrails are not inside the model. They are ordinary code around it, in four layers:

GuardWhat it doesAttack it stops in the lab
Input guardRemoves instructions hidden inside data (tickets, emails, documents)Attack 1
Policy guardBusiness rules written as code — the AI cannot talk its way past themAttack 1
Output guardMasks names, phone numbers and emails before anything leavesAttack 2
Action guardAnything that touches a customer or a plant needs a human to approveAttack 3

4 · Evals and Observability Layers 6 and 7

In Lab 3 you judged one scenario at a time, by eye. That does not scale. An eval runs a fixed golden set of cases — with known correct answers — every time anything changes: a new model, a new prompt, a new document. It produces one scorecard. Three kinds of checks go into it:

  • Exact checks in code — was the verdict right? did a phone number leave? was an email sent?
  • An AI judge — a second model scores things code cannot, like clarity. Cheap, but spot-check the judge by hand.
  • Human review — for anything that touches a customer or a plant.

Observability answers a different question: what did the AI actually do, step by step, and what did it cost? In production this is your early-warning system.

8 · Proving AI value — with no baseline Layer 10

New project, no history, no one-to-one comparison. You do not need history — you build the comparison inside the project:

MethodHowMeasures
Estimate first, then measureThe team estimates each task "without AI" in planning, as usual, then records the actual time with AI. Report the median over 20+ tasks.Time
Split tasks inside the projectSimilar tasks alternate with and without AI for two sprintsTime and quality
Cost per unit of workCost per test case, per requirement, per defect found — including AI spend, review time and learning timeCost
Quality as a gateSpeed counts only if escaped defects, rework % and change failure rate do not get worse. The first two sprints are your baseline.Quality

The formulas: Time saved = (estimate without AI − actual with AI) ÷ estimate, reported as a range. · Net cost = hours saved × loaded rate − (licences + tokens + extra review + training). · Quality = escaped defects and rework % against sprints 1–2.

9 · AI and cyber security Layer 9

  • Securing AI — the five OWASP risks for AI applications a manager must know: prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure, supply chain (models and plug-ins you did not build), excessive agency, and over-reliance on confident wrong answers.
  • Shadow AI — ABB data pasted into public tools. Give people an approved tool (Abby) so they do not need an unapproved one.
  • Plant (OT) side — AI near control systems follows IEC 62443 zones and conduits. AI reads from OT; it never writes to OT without a human.
  • AI-written code — secrets in code and vulnerable libraries arrive faster. Keep security scanning gates in every pipeline.
  • AI for defenders — triaging vulnerability advisories and PSAs, checking the software bill of materials, summarising logs.
