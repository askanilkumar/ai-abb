M1 · Where AI runs — the three tiers

Whenever your team uses AI, it is using one of three options. We call them tiers. The tier you pick decides how you pay, where your data goes, and how much your own team has to build and maintain. Many poor AI decisions happen simply because a team picked the wrong tier for the job.

Tier 1 · Ready-made AI toolsTier 2 · Cloud model servicesTier 3 · Run it yourself
WhatTools like Abby and Copilot that you simply log in to and useAI models you call from your own application through the cloud, paid per use. Today's labs use this (Azure AI Foundry).Open-source models running on your own servers
You controlVery little. You can only change what you type.The instructions, the documents the model sees, the safety checks, and which model handles which taskEverything, including the model itself
Cost modelA fixed fee per user per month (a per-user licence)Pay for what you use, counted in tokensPay for the servers by the hour, whether you use them or not
Data pathAs per the tool vendor's termsStays in your company's own cloud account and regionNever leaves your own network
Right forHelping one person work fasterTeam workflows and AI inside your products. This covers most cases.Very strict data rules, or very heavy usage all day, every day
Wrong whenYou need AI inside a team process or a productSystems that must stay fully offline, or extremely heavy usageYou do not have a team to run and maintain the servers

The decision tree for "where should this AI run?" This is your first take-home tool. An editable copy is in the course files. Use these four questions whenever your team brings you a new AI request:

1 · Is this about one person working faster, or about a process the whole team uses?

If it is one person, use Tier 1, that is Abby or Copilot. You can stop here. There is no need to build something when a per-user tool the company already pays for does the job.

2 · Is this data allowed to go to a cloud AI service in your company's cloud account?

If the data cannot leave your own network at all, you need Tier 3. Otherwise, go to the next question.

3 · Will it be used very heavily and steadily, for example millions of requests, all day, every day?

If yes, compare the cost of Tier 3 with Tier 2 before you decide. If no, or if usage goes up and down, or if you do not know yet, choose Tier 2.

4 · When in doubt

Start with Tier 2. Begin with a cloud model service, measure the real usage and cost, and move only when the numbers tell you to. All of today's labs run on Tier 2.

Where does Abby fit? Abby gives ABB employees access to Claude and GPT models, so these model families are already approved for use here. But Abby is a Tier 1 tool for individuals. It cannot plug into your team's own tools, it cannot search your team's documents in a controlled way, and you cannot measure its answer quality with a test set. Filling exactly this gap is the job of Tier 2, and that is what we build today.

M2 · Prompting for cost and quality

A prompt is the instruction you give to the model. This session is not about prompt basics. It is about writing prompts for real applications that run thousands of times a day, where every choice in the prompt shows up in the monthly bill.

Good practiceWhat it meansWhy it matters to you as a manager
Ask for a fixed formatAsk the model to fill a fixed format (for example JSON fields) instead of writing free paragraphsSoftware can read and test the answer easily, and shorter answers cost less
Give the right factsInclude the reference facts the task needs, and nothing moreA model given the facts is more accurate than one that has to guess. But every extra page you send is paid for, even if it does not help.
Keep the fixed part firstPut the instructions that never change at the start, and the part that changes (the question) at the endProviders remember ("cache") a repeated starting text and charge much less for it. This only works when the start is exactly the same every time.
Help the small modelSmall models need clear rules and a few examples; large models need fewerGood rules and examples let the much cheaper small model do the job. This is where most of the savings come from.
The habit to stop: the "kitchen sink" prompt. This is a long, 300-word paragraph of polite instructions that tries to cover every possible case. It costs several times more than a short prompt with a fixed format, it never gets the caching discount, and it usually gives worse results. You will see this for yourself in the next lab.

13:25 – 13:50 · trainer-led

M3 · Retrieval done properly

RAG stands for "retrieval-augmented generation". In simple words, it means look it up first, then answer. The system finds the right passages in your documents, gives them to the model along with the question, and the model answers with references to its sources. There are four stages, and each one has a setting that teams usually get wrong the first time:

StageThe settingThe common mistake
Cut the documents into piecesHow big each piece ("chunk") isPieces that are too big: the search becomes vague, and every question costs several times more tokens
Turn pieces into numbersWhich embedding model to useThis is paid once, when documents are added or changed, not for every question. Teams often worry and overspend here.
Find the right piecesHow many pieces to fetch (called "k")Too few: an answer that needs two documents gets only one. Too many: you pay for text that does not help.
Write the answerThe instructions to the modelWithout the rule "answer only from the documents given", the model fills the gaps with confident guesses
The line to take home: the model is the engine, and your documents and how you search them are the fuel. When a RAG pilot gives poor answers, the fix is almost never a bigger model. It is usually the piece size, the search settings or the instructions. In the next lab you will break and fix all three.

M4 · Guardrails and evaluation

Your assistant now works. The next question, from compliance and from your own judgement, is simple: can we trust it, and can we prove it? There are two kinds of protection. Both are cheap, and most teams still skip them:

ProtectionWhat it doesWhat it costs
Check the question (input guardrail)Stops off-topic questions and attempts to trick the AI before they reach it. Simple rules come first, then a quick check by a cheap model.A tiny fraction of a cent per question
Check the answer (output guardrail)Checks that every statement in the answer is backed by the documents that were found. It catches made-up answers before a technician acts on them.One extra call to a cheap model
Test set and scoring (eval)A set of questions with known good answers. A model scores the assistant's answers, so quality becomes a number you can track.A few minutes to run
Score, change, score againTake a score before any change and again after it, so nothing gets worse without you noticing. Your teams already do this regression testing for code.Only the habit
Your rule as a manager, starting tomorrow: "No test set, no launch." This one sentence changes every AI discussion in your team from opinions to measured numbers.

M5 · When retrieval is not enough

In Lab 3 one question could not be answered: "Which sites have F-07 tickets on drives affected by SB-2025-04, and what single action fixes all of them?" To answer it, the system has to combine three things: the tickets, the bulletin and the firmware release notes. Simple RAG searches for similar text, so it cannot do this. A knowledge graph, or even a plain database query (SQL), can answer it by following the links between tickets, drives and bulletins.

The rule to take home for choosing between them:

If your users' questions look like thisThen build this
"What does this manual say?" or "How do I fix this fault?" The answer is written in a passage somewhere.Simple RAG. A knowledge graph would add cost without improving the answers.
Questions that link several things together, like "which customers, on which firmware, are hit by which bulletin"A knowledge graph, or quite often just a normal database query (SQL)
You are not sure yetStart with simple RAG, record the questions it fails on and count them. That count tells you whether a knowledge graph is worth building.
