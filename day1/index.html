M1 · Where AI runs — the three tiers

Every AI request your team makes lands in one of three tiers. The tier decides the cost model, the data exposure, and how much engineering you own. Most bad AI decisions are tier mismatches.

Tier 1 · SaaS assistantsTier 2 · Managed endpointsTier 3 · Self-hosted
WhatAbby, Basic Copilot — someone else's appServerless model APIs (Azure AI Foundry — what your VM uses today)Open models on your own compute
You controlAlmost nothing — prompts onlyPrompts, context, guardrails, routingEverything, including weights
Cost modelPer seat / bundledPer token — scales with usePer GPU-hour — scales with capacity
Data pathVendor's termsYour tenancy, your regionNever leaves your network
Right forIndividual productivityTeam applications — the 80% caseHard data constraints, high steady volume
Wrong whenYou need it in a workflow or productAir-gapped or extreme-volume workloadsYou have no platform team for it

The deployment decision tree — your first takeaway framework. It lives in the repo as an editable template; use it on any request your team brings you:

1 · Is this one person being faster, or a team workflow?

One person → Tier 1 (Abby / Copilot). Done — don't build what a seat licence solves.

2 · Can the data leave your tenancy under vendor terms?

No, and it can't even sit in your cloud region → Tier 3. Otherwise continue.

3 · Is volume steady and very high (always-on, millions of calls)?

Yes → price Tier 3 against Tier 2 before committing. No / spiky / unknown → Tier 2.

4 · Default

Tier 2. Start serverless, measure, move only with evidence. Today's labs run here.

Abby's place in this picture: Abby fronts Claude and GPT models inside ABB — that tells you the model families are already accepted here. What Abby can't give your team is a workflow: no API into your tools, no custom retrieval over your documents, no evals. That gap is exactly what Tier 2 fills, and exactly what you build today.

M2 · Prompting for cost and quality

Not prompt-engineering basics — you've had those. This is prompting as a production design discipline, where every design choice shows up on the invoice.

PatternWhat it isWhy a manager cares
Structured outputDemand JSON against a schema, not proseParseable, testable, pipeline-ready — and shorter outputs cost less
Context engineeringSupply the reference facts the task needs, nothing moreFacts in context beat facts hoped-for in training; extra context is paid noise
Cache-friendly designStable system prompt, variable part lastProviders price cached input tokens at a fraction — but only identical, stable prefixes cache
Model-appropriate scaffoldingMini models need rules and examples; frontier models need lessScaffolding is what makes the 20×-cheaper model usable — it's where routing savings come from
The anti-pattern to ban: the "kitchen sink" prompt — 300 words of politely-worded prose instructions, rewritten per call. It costs several times more than a tight schema prompt, caches never, and usually scores worse. You will prove this yourself in about ten minutes.

Caching only works on stable, identical prefixes — a prompt that changes every call never caches. Design decision → invoice line.

3. A mini model fails a task a frontier model passes. The first engineering response is:

V3/V4 scaffolding is what unlocked the cheap model in Lab 2. Route up only when scaffolding measurably fails — that discipline is most of your AI budget.

13:15 – 13:45 · trainer-led

M3 · Retrieval done properly

RAG — retrieval-augmented generation — is how the model gets your knowledge: find the relevant passages, hand them to the model as context, answer with citations. Four stages, each with a dial your team will set wrong the first time:

StageThe dialThe classic failure
ChunkChunk size & overlapWhole-document chunks: retrieval gets vague, every question costs 5× in tokens
EmbedEmbedding modelPaid once per document update — not per question. Teams overspend here out of fear
Retrievek (how many chunks)k too small: answers that need two documents get one. k too big: paid noise
AnswerSystem promptNo "answer only from context" rule → the model fills gaps from training data, confidently
The sentence to take home: the model is the engine; your knowledge pipeline is the fuel quality. When a RAG pilot disappoints, the fix is almost never a bigger model — it's chunking, retrieval and prompt rules. You'll break and fix all three in the next hour.

M4 · Guardrails and evaluation

Your assistant works. Now the question your compliance and your own judgment will ask: can it be trusted, and can you prove it? Two mechanisms, both cheap, both skipped by most teams:

MechanismWhat it doesCost
Input guardrailBounces off-topic questions and injection attempts at the door — rules first, cheap model check secondFractions of a cent per request
Output guardrailGroundedness check: is every claim in the answer supported by the retrieved context? Catches confident fabrication before a technician acts on itOne cheap-model call
Eval set + judge20 questions with reference answers, scored by an LLM judge. Quality becomes a numberMinutes to run
Baseline → change → re-scoreRegression protection — the discipline your teams already apply to code, applied to AIThe habit is the cost
Your sentence as a manager, starting tomorrow: "No eval set, no deployment." It is the single highest-leverage sentence in this programme — it converts every AI argument in your team from opinion to measurement.

M5 · When retrieval is not enough

The multi-hop question Lab 3 could not answer — "which sites have F-07 tickets on drives affected by SB-2025-04, and what one action closes all of them?" — needs a join: tickets × bulletin × release notes. On the projector you'll watch flat RAG fail it, then a knowledge graph answer it.

The decision rule you take away (the demo exists only to make this rule stick):

Signal in your use caseBuild
Questions are "what does X say / how do I fix Y" — answers live in passagesFlat RAG. A graph adds cost and no accuracy
Questions join entities — "which customers × which firmware × which bulletin"Graph-backed retrieval, or honestly: sometimes just SQL
Not sureStart flat, log the questions that fail, count them. The failures tell you if a graph pays
